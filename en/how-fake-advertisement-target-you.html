How Do Scam Ads Target You? How Ad Targeting Works and How to Stay Safe

SafePhone · Updated September 27, 2026

Most of the revenue behind social networks and free software comes from advertising. Ads themselves are harmless, but cybercriminals exploit their massive, easy reach to find “prey”. Scam ads impersonate well-known brands, play on emotions and greed, and are aimed precisely at people who need something right now. This article explains how ad targeting works, the messages scam ads commonly use, the warning signs, and how to protect yourself.

Common types of scam ads

Free platforms are designed to draw users' attention to ads to boost views and revenue. Scammers take advantage of this to target Internet users who don't have much experience with online fraud. Some examples:

Fake banking, stock trading, and crypto app ads

These ads use the name, logo, and images of a stock trading, cryptocurrency, or banking app, along with an invitation to “install and claim your reward.” In reality, the software is info-stealing malware (grabbing passwords and bank accounts) or ransomware. See types of malware.

Fake restaurant, hotel, and resort ads

These ads use the name, logo, and images of a well-known restaurant, hotel, or resort, with deals that are “limited time, limited quantity.” Click through and you land on a fake website copied exactly from the official one. Once you pay a deposit or transfer money using the details on that page, the money goes straight to the scammers, who then vanish and can't be reached. These sites often come with fake Facebook pages to look more credible.

“Your money is stuck”: the lure to install another app

In many cases, after receiving the money, the scammers pretend they haven't received it, claim the “money is stuck,” and lure the victim into installing another app to “push” the payment through. In reality, this app lets the attackers control the phone remotely and then take over the victim's bank account.

Copycat versions of popular apps

Some ads promote copycats of ordinary apps, like math learning or music apps. If you look closely, you'll see they request many unnecessary permissions, a sign the app may be used to eavesdrop, steal data, or encrypt data for ransom. See how to spot malicious apps.

These ads are usually professionally designed, eye-catching, and copy brands so convincingly that they're very hard to tell apart from real ads. Their goals are usually to:

  • Trick you into transferring money
  • Steal your accounts
  • Collect personal information
  • Spread malware

Why do fake ads show up at just the right time?

Online ad systems have long tracked user behavior through free tools, using it to guess each person's needs and show matching ads. When you:

  • Search for products
  • Watch review videos
  • Read related articles
  • Look up financial or investment information

…the system learns that you're interested in that topic. Related ads then appear anywhere connected to the advertiser: while you scroll social media, listen to music, watch videos, or even inside other apps. This is the ad revenue-sharing model between platforms. One of the most common tracking tools is the cookie. See what cookies are and how websites track you.

However, not every ad network can detect fake or malicious content, especially ads that lead to fake websites and apps. Scammers exploit these gaps in ad review, combined with techniques like showing “clean” content to reviewers and scam content to real users. See 12 camouflage techniques scam websites use.

How “targeting” works in online advertising

Bad actors can run ads aimed at specific user groups based on demographics and interests, for example:

  • People looking for a loan
  • People looking for a job
  • People interested in investing
  • People who want to buy a cheap phone

That's why fake ads seem to match your needs so well. What you search on Google, what you post on Facebook, even how long you pause on a post, can all be recorded and analyzed by platforms to categorize your interests. Scammers don't need to know who you are. They just pick the right audience, and the ad system delivers the ad straight to your screen.

Example: you just searched Google for “vay tiền nhanh” (“quick loan”). A few hours later, Facebook shows you an ad saying “Vay 50 triệu không thế chấp, giải ngân trong 5 phút” (“Borrow 50 million VND with no collateral, paid out in 5 minutes”) from a page using a logo that looks like a bank's. That's not a coincidence. That's targeting.

4 types of messages common in fake ads

To lure viewers into downloading malicious apps or paying upfront, fake ads usually play on greed and the fear of missing out:

Type of adCommon messagesConsequences
High-return investmentsUnusual profits, “risk-free” guarantees, fast money, “today only” opportunitiesLosing money to fake trading platforms and bogus projects
Excessive discountsPhones at rock-bottom prices, designer goods with unusually deep discounts, high-value vouchersNever receiving the goods, or receiving counterfeits
Fake account updatesRequests to log in again, verify your account, or update your bank detailsPasswords stolen through a fake website
Fake softwareFake antivirus apps, cracked software, device “speed boosters”Malware infection, data harvesting

Many of these messages overlap with the list of common scam tactics in 2026, such as “lợi nhuận cam kết” (“guaranteed returns”) and “việc nhẹ lương cao” (“easy work, high pay”).

Why do so many people fall for scam ads?

Fake ads usually:

  • Are very professionally designed
  • Appear right when you need something
  • Play on strong emotions
  • Create a sense of urgency
  • Use celebrity images or fake reviews, sometimes even deepfake videos (see advanced scam techniques)

When you're actively interested in a product or service, you're more likely to let your guard down.

Warning signs of a fake ad

Be especially careful when you see or hear the following. When an easy win comes with a “limited time” tag, it's very likely a scam. Don't download an app, hand over information, or pay a deposit just because of an ad.

  • Unusually low prices
  • Returns that seem too good to be true
  • Pressure to transfer money quickly
  • Time pressure
  • Links to unfamiliar websites with domains that don't match the brand
  • Invitations to download an app via a link (APK file) instead of from Google Play or the App Store
  • Lots of spelling mistakes
  • No clear business information
  • The page running the ad is newly created or has changed its name many times

Tip: for ads on Facebook, you can tap the name of the page running the ad to view its Page transparency section, or look it up in Meta's Ad Library to see what ads that page is running.

How to avoid scam ads

1. Check the website's domain carefully

Always check the domain before entering any information. See how to check if a website is a scam. The quickest way is to use SafePhone: the app keeps a list of official domains for popular services and products, so if a website deliberately impersonates a brand, SafePhone can detect it and warn you.

2. Don't trust deals that are too good to be true

  • Prices that are too low usually come with risks
  • Returns that are too high are usually unrealistic

3. Don't give out sensitive information

A website opened from an ad that asks for your banking password or OTP code is almost certainly fake. If you need to log in, open the official app yourself or type the web address yourself. Never log in through an ad link.

4. Only download apps from official stores

Download apps from Google Play or the App Store, and check the developer name and the permissions the app requests. Don't install APK files sent through ad links or messages.

On Facebook, tap the three-dot icon in the corner of the ad → Report ad; on Google, use the report option under the info icon next to the ad (menu names may vary by version). Every report helps get the ad taken down sooner for everyone else.

What to do if you clicked a scam ad

  1. You only opened the page and didn't enter anything: close the page and clear your browsing history; the risk is usually low.
  2. You entered a password, OTP, or card number: immediately call your bank's official hotline to freeze your account and card; change your password and turn on two-factor authentication.
  3. You installed an app from the ad: disconnect from the network, uninstall the app, check special permissions (accessibility, device admin), and have someone with technical expertise check your phone.
  4. You transferred money: contact your bank right away, keep evidence (screenshots of the ad, website, messages, receipts), and report it to the police.

Conclusion

Fake ads are growing more sophisticated and use modern ad systems themselves to reach exactly the right targets. Understanding how they work makes you more alert, and knowing how to spot fake ads has become an important part of staying safe online. FAQ

Why do I see scam ads for exactly what I'm looking for?

Ad systems record your behavior, such as searches, videos watched, and articles read, to guess what you need. Scammers only have to pick the right audience, like people looking for loans or jobs, and the ads get delivered to you.

How can I spot fake ads on Facebook?

Be wary of unusually low prices, returns that seem too good to be true, time pressure, links to unfamiliar domains, invitations to download apps outside official stores, and ad-running pages that are newly created or have changed names many times.

Are ads on Facebook and Google safe since they've been reviewed?

Not necessarily. Ad review doesn't catch every fake ad, and scammers use cloaking techniques to show clean content to reviewers. Always check the domain and never enter sensitive information through an ad link.

Is there a way to hide ads so I don't tap them by mistake?

Yes. SafePhone lets you turn on blocking for Facebook ads and Google Ads, and it hides sponsored results when you search with SafePhone. You can also use Android's Private DNS feature with an ad-blocking service.

What should I do if I installed an app from an ad?

Disconnect from the network, uninstall the app, check special permissions such as accessibility or device admin, change your banking passwords from a different device, and contact your bank if you see any unusual transactions.
