Advanced Scams: Deepfakes, Fake Bank and Police Calls, and How to Protect Yourself

SafePhone · Updated September 27, 2026

Online scams used to mean misspelled text messages, calls from unknown numbers or links that were obviously suspicious. Today, advanced scams use AI, deepfakes, voice cloning, fake video calls, and impersonation of banks and government agencies. Some scammers even build a relationship over months just to set up one final con. This article explains the 3 most dangerous types, their warning signs, and how to protect yourself and your family.

Why do careful people still fall for advanced scams?

The frightening part is that many victims are not uninformed at all. Some run businesses, use technology every day and are genuinely careful, yet they are still psychologically manipulated and lose money. Today's scams don't just exploit technical weaknesses; they exploit emotions, trust, panic and the urge to fix a problem quickly.

Modern scams usually combine several things at once:

  • AI, deepfakes and voice cloning
  • Spoofed phone numbers and caller names
  • Psychological manipulation, known as social engineering

Victims usually don't lose money because they lack knowledge, but because they are pushed into an overwhelming emotional state: fear, worry for a loved one, or extreme urgency. Many scripts reuse familiar phrases; see our list of common scam tactics in 2026.

Deepfakes and AI voice cloning of loved ones

One of the most dangerous tactics today is using AI to fake a real person's face and voice. From just a few videos or voice recordings posted publicly on Facebook, TikTok or YouTube, AI can imitate:

  • How a person talks
  • Their distinctive voice and intonation
  • Their facial expressions while speaking
  • How their lips and eyes move

Scammers use these to create deepfake videos or fake video calls. The victim sees a familiar face and hears a familiar voice, so they easily believe it's a relative or friend. The scammers usually create an intense sense of urgency, with lines like these (the Vietnamese you may actually hear, followed by the English meaning):

  • “Anh đang có việc gấp, chuyển tiền giúp anh ngay” (“I've got an emergency, send me the money right now”)
  • “Anh đang bận họp nên không nói lâu được” (“I'm in a meeting so I can't talk long”)
  • “Em chuyển tạm trước đi rồi anh giải thích sau” (“Just send it for now, I'll explain later”)
  • “Anh đang gặp sự cố cần xử lý ngay lập tức” (“I've run into a problem that needs sorting out immediately”)

Under pressure, especially when a loved one seems to be involved, we tend to react emotionally before we have time to think logically. That is exactly what scammers exploit.

Signs it might be a deepfake

  • The call is very short and keeps piling on time pressure
  • The other person avoids talking for long or answering complicated questions
  • Lip movements sometimes don't fully match the audio
  • The video feels slightly “off”: unnatural movement, blurring or stuttering when the head turns
  • Odd details about clothing, location or circumstances
  • An excuse for why they can't make a normal phone call and can only text or call through a social media account

Note: deepfakes are getting more sophisticated, so not seeing any warning signs doesn't mean the call is safe. The surest method is to verify through another channel.

How to protect yourself from deepfake voice scams

  • Never verify someone's identity by video call or voice alone
  • Hang up and call back on the person's real phone number (the one you have saved, not the number that just called you)
  • Ask about a shared memory or private event; AI can fake a face and voice, but scammers usually don't know these personal details
  • Ask about other family members to test them
  • Agree on a “family password” in advance: a word or question only your family knows, to use when someone calls asking for money urgently
  • Be especially wary of any urgent request to transfer money
  • Limit public videos with clear recordings of your and your family's voices; set your profiles to private

Trust-building scams that run for months

This type is even more dangerous because it doesn't happen in a few minutes; it is prepared over a long time. The scammer approaches the victim like an ordinary person:

  • Adding them as a friend on social media
  • Buying from them a few times to build credibility
  • Chatting in a friendly way, like a real business partner
  • Interacting regularly to create a sense of familiarity

Only after weeks or months do they bring up “big business opportunities,” such as:

  • A project to install computers in schools
  • A contract to supply equipment to a government agency
  • A large order with very high profits

Because trust has already been built, the victim no longer has their initial guard up. The scammer then starts steering them:

  • Asking them to pay money upfront
  • Transferring money to “another partner”
  • Bundling invoices or routing transactions in circles
  • Making everything seem reasonable and professional

In the end, once the money arrives, the whole setup vanishes. Social media accounts and fake Facebook pages of businesses or agencies are often used to make the story more convincing.

Why this type of scam works

  • It exploits trust between acquaintances
  • Victims think someone they know has already vouched for it
  • The story and paperwork sound very logical
  • Attractive profits make many people drop their guard
  • The process feels natural, so it's hard to suspect

How to protect yourself

  • Don't trust someone completely just because “a friend introduced them”
  • Verify companies and clients yourself through independent sources (look up their tax ID, call a publicly listed hotline, check the official website; see how to check if a website is a scam)
  • Don't transfer money to a third party without checking carefully
  • Be careful with any deal that requires you to pay upfront
  • When profits look too easy, be more cautious than usual

Impersonating banks, police and government agencies

A very common tactic today is impersonating banks, the police, the tax authority or mobile carriers. Combined with technical tricks such as fake BTS stations (fake cell towers that broadcast SMS under a brand name) or caller ID spoofing, messages and calls can display names that look very official:

  • Bank names and hotline names
  • Bộ Công an (Vietnam's Ministry of Public Security), Cục Thuế (the General Department of Taxation)
  • Mobile carriers

This makes many people believe the contact is genuine. Scammers then create a frightening situation:

  • Your bank account is being hacked
  • You're linked to a money-laundering case
  • Your SIM card is about to be locked
  • There's an unusual transaction that needs to be verified right away

Once the victim panics, the scammers instruct them to read out an OTP code, install an unfamiliar app (often containing malware; see how to spot malicious apps), log in to a fake website, or transfer money to “verify the account.” Following just a few steps can be enough for the bank account to be taken over.

The most important rules:
  • Never read an OTP to anyone over the phone. Banks and government agencies have no such procedure.
  • Never transfer money to “verify” or “secure your account.” No agency asks for this.
  • If in doubt, hang up and call the official hotline yourself.
  • Don't fully trust the name or number shown on your phone screen.

The most important rules for staying safe

  • Always verify information through several different sources
  • Never transfer money while under psychological pressure; stop for at least a few minutes and ask someone else
  • Don't fully trust a video call or a voice
  • Never give your OTP or password to anyone
  • Don't tap suspicious links in SMS or email
  • Be careful with opportunities that seem “too good to be true”

Protecting parents and older adults from advanced scams

Older adults are often the most targeted group. A few things you can do right now:

  • Share this article and agree on a “family password”
  • Tell your parents: if anyone calls asking for money urgently, even if it sounds like their child or grandchild, call back the saved number before transferring anything
  • Install SafePhone and turn on blocking calls from numbers not in contacts so only people they know can get through
  • Set up alerts sent to you when your parents get a call from an unknown number
  • See also apps for older adults

What to do if you've been scammed

  1. Call your bank's official hotline immediately (the number printed on the back of your card) to freeze your account and card and ask for help with the transfer you just made.
  2. Change your passwords for banking, email and social media; turn on two-factor authentication.
  3. If you installed an unfamiliar app: disconnect from the internet, uninstall the app and have an expert check your device.
  4. Keep evidence: messages, phone numbers, the recipient's account number, receipts.
  5. Report it to the nearest police station. Be wary of anyone who later offers to “recover your lost money.”

Conclusion

Technology makes life more convenient, but it also enables scams that are more sophisticated than ever. A few minutes of letting your guard down can cost someone years of savings. What matters most is not only using security tools but also staying informed and alert, and sharing this information with your loved ones, especially older family members.

FAQ

What are advanced (high-tech) scams?

They are scams that combine technology such as AI, deepfakes, voice cloning, spoofed phone numbers or caller names with psychological manipulation, to get victims to transfer money, read out OTP codes or install malicious apps.

How can I spot a deepfake video call?

Watch for short calls, constant pressure, lip movements that don't match the audio, unnatural motion or an implausible situation. However, deepfakes are getting harder to spot by eye, so the surest method is to hang up and call the person back on their real phone number.

Will my bank ever call and ask me to read out an OTP code?

No. Banks and government agencies never ask customers to read OTP codes over the phone or transfer money to verify an account. If you get such a request, hang up and call the official hotline yourself.

If my phone shows the bank's name, is the message or call genuine?

Not necessarily. Caller names and numbers can be spoofed, for example through fake BTS stations (fake cell towers) that broadcast SMS under a brand name. Don't trust the display name alone; contact the organization yourself through official channels.

What is a family password and how do I use it?

It is a secret word or question that only your family knows, agreed on in advance and never posted online. When someone calls claiming to be a relative and urgently asks for money, ask for this password; a scammer using a deepfake won't be able to answer.
