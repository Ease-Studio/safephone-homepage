9 Habits That Expose Your Personal Data and How to Fix Them

SafePhone · Updated September 27, 2026

In the digital age, personal data is one of your most valuable assets. Yet many people end up with their personal data exposed not because of a sophisticated hack, but because of seemingly harmless everyday habits. Here are 9 common habits that make your data easy to steal — weak passwords, public WiFi, clicking unknown links, skipping 2FA… — and how to fix each one right away.

1. Using weak passwords or the same password everywhere

This is the most common personal security mistake. In password leaks, a surprising number of accounts use extremely simple passwords like 123456 or password. Many others base their passwords on personal details (name, date of birth, phone number), which makes them easy to guess.

Hackers have ready-made tools that guess passwords by trying huge numbers of combinations — from lists of common passwords and leaked passwords to variations built from your personal information (brute-force and dictionary attacks). If you also reuse the same password across sites, a single breach puts every other account at risk.

How to fix it:

  • The longer the password, the harder it is to guess: aim for 12 characters or more, or use a long, memorable passphrase made of several random words.
  • Leave out personal information; mix uppercase and lowercase letters, numbers, and special characters.
  • Use a unique password for every account — especially email and banking.
  • Use a password manager so you don't have to remember them all.

2. Saving passwords in the browser and on public computers

Chrome and Firefox offer to save your passwords for convenience. But this habit comes with a few risks:

  • If the browser has an undisclosed (zero-day) vulnerability, in the worst case hackers could exploit it to grab your saved passwords. Info-stealing malware on computers also commonly targets passwords stored in the browser.
  • On someone else's computer — an internet café, a print shop, or a shared machine at work — the next person can use your saved password to log in to your account.

How to fix it: never save passwords on a shared computer, and always log out as soon as you're done. On your own devices, memorize your passwords or use an encrypted password manager unlocked with face or fingerprint authentication on your phone — secure, and you won't forget them.

3. Connecting to unsafe public WiFi

Free WiFi in coffee shops and airports is often poorly secured. Common problems include:

  • Weak encryption: if the network details show the WiFi uses WEP or the original WPA, don't connect — both standards have been broken and there are plenty of tools for eavesdropping on them. Today you should use networks encrypted with WPA2 or higher, ideally WPA3. Open networks without a password don't encrypt data between your phone and the router either.
  • Evil Twin attacks: a scammer broadcasts a WiFi network with the same name as the one you mean to join; if you connect to the wrong one, they can monitor your online activity or steal your login details.
  • Data-harvesting surveys: some WiFi networks ask you to fill in personal information in the form of a survey. You can usually skip this step and still connect.

How to fix it: ask staff for the exact WiFi name, use 4G/5G for banking, and turn off auto-reconnect for public networks.

4. Clicking unknown links (phishing)

Phishing is the most common way information gets stolen today: scammers exploit psychological weak spots to trick users into handing over their information or downloading malware. Typical scenarios:

  • Emails impersonating your bank.
  • Messages saying you've won a prize.
  • Fake login links for Facebook, WhatsApp, banks, and e-wallets.

5. Installing apps from unknown sources

Apps downloaded outside official sources may contain info-stealing malware. Scammers often disguise malware as useful free software or as cracked versions of expensive, well-known programs. Trusting software from unreliable sources can cost you your personal data, or even lead to a ransomware attack — see more on types of malware.

How to fix it: only download apps from Google Play, the App Store, or the publisher's official website. If you're not sure which site is official, paste the address into SafePhone to check its reputation before downloading. On your phone, see our detailed guide on how to spot malicious apps.

6. Oversharing on social media

Many people spend more time on Facebook, TikTok, X, and the like than in real life. Posting overly detailed personal information can be used against you: scammers collect your name, where you live, your family and friends, your routines and interests to trick you or people you know into sending money or installing malware — see common scam tactics in 2026.

Worse, today's AI can generate fake photos and videos (deepfakes), including explicit content, from just a few photos of your face. Exposing too many personal photos increases the risk of your image being misused for defamation, fraud, or extortion.

How to fix it:

  • Limit what you share about your private life: home address, your kids' school, travel plans, photos of ID documents, boarding passes with booking codes.
  • Turn on profile lock so strangers can't see your information.
  • Set posts and your friends list to “Friends” visibility.

7. Not turning on two-factor authentication (2FA)

Gmail, Facebook, X, and many popular apps all offer two-factor authentication. When there's a new sign-in, the service asks for an extra verification step — an OTP code sent by SMS, a code from an authenticator app, or face or fingerprint confirmation on a device that's already signed in. That way, even if a scammer figures out your password, they still can't take over your account.

However, 2FA is off by default in many apps. How to fix it: review your important accounts (email, social media, e-wallets) and turn on 2FA as soon as possible; when you have the choice, prefer an authenticator app or on-device confirmation over SMS. And remember: never read your OTP code to anyone, including someone claiming to be a bank employee.

8. Skipping software updates and using cracked software

Outdated software often contains known but unpatched security holes, which can be a gateway for hackers to get into your device. Many people think updates are only about new features, a nicer interface, or better performance. In reality, the most important part is patching security vulnerabilities. Each update typically:

  • Fixes security bugs that have been discovered.
  • Blocks new attack methods.
  • Hardens the system against threats already circulating in the wild.

Not updating means you're running a version known to have vulnerabilities — and hackers know it too. In many cases, simply opening the wrong image or music file, or visiting an unfamiliar website, is enough to get malware installed through these holes. How to fix it: turn on automatic updates for your operating system and apps, and avoid cracked software — cracks are also a common way malware gets installed.

9. Not controlling app permissions

Many apps collect more data than they need, but most users ignore it. On Google Play and the App Store, every app must disclose the data it collects and the system permissions it uses — yet we usually skip over this and tap “Allow” on everything. That habit is no different from signing away your personal information and private life to app developers.

How to fix it: check permissions before installing. If an app asks for too many permissions, overly deep access, or permissions unrelated to its main features, hold off — look for reviews, ask someone who knows security, or pick an alternative app. Periodically go to Settings → Privacy to revoke permissions from apps you rarely use (menu names may vary by version). Websites also track you in their own way — see what cookies are.

What to do if you suspect your personal data has leaked

  1. Immediately change the password of the affected account and every account that shares that password, starting with your main email.
  2. Turn on 2FA and sign out of all unfamiliar devices in the account's security settings.
  3. If your bank is involved, call its official hotline to lock your card and account.
  4. Warn family and friends to watch out for messages impersonating you.
  5. Be wary of calls and messages offering to “help recover your account” — scammers often target people whose data has just leaked. You can use how to block unknown and scam numbers to filter them out.
  6. Report to the police if you've lost money or are being extorted.

These habits are just one part of a bigger picture — see our roundup of common online threats.

Conclusion

The habits that leave your personal data exposed are usually small, but their long-term consequences can be huge. Recognizing these behaviors helps you become more security-aware and avoid unnecessary risks online. Start with three things today: change any reused passwords, turn on 2FA for your email, and review the permissions of the apps on your phone.

FAQ

Which habits are most likely to expose personal data?

The most common are using weak or reused passwords, saving passwords on public computers, using public WiFi with weak encryption, clicking unknown links, installing apps from unknown sources, oversharing on social media, not turning on 2FA, not updating software, and granting apps every permission.

How strong does a password need to be?

A password should be at least 12 characters long or be a long passphrase, contain no personal information, mix uppercase and lowercase letters, numbers, and special characters, and never be reused across accounts. A password manager means you don't have to remember them all.

Is it safe to save passwords in Chrome?

Never on a shared computer. On a personal device the risk is lower, but saved passwords can still be stolen by malware or through security holes; an encrypted password manager protected by biometric authentication is a safer choice.

What is two-factor authentication and do I need it?

Two-factor authentication adds an extra verification step, such as an OTP code or face confirmation, when you sign in on a new device. It's essential, because even if your password leaks, a scammer still can't take over your account.

Which WiFi encryption standard is safe?

Use networks with WPA2 or higher, ideally WPA3. Avoid networks using WEP or the original WPA, since these standards have been broken, and be careful with open networks or networks with duplicate names.
