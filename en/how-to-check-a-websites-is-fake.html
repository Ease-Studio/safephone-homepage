Common types of scam websites

Many websites deliberately impersonate trusted brands to trick people into entering personal information, bank details or passwords. The most common types:

  • Fake banks and e-wallets: ask you to log in so they can steal your password and OTP codes.
  • Fake delivery companies, restaurants and hotels: run ads to lure customers into ordering or booking, then disappear once the bank transfer arrives.
  • Fake stock, bond and crypto exchanges: promise high returns to get victims to deposit money — see also security apps for crypto traders.
  • Fake software download sites: pose as Word, Excel, games, even security software or VPNs, but actually contain malware — see how to detect malicious apps.
  • Reward surveys and fake job listings: advertised widely to harvest personal information.

Spotting fake websites early is the best way to protect yourself and the people around you, especially children and older family members.

9 signs of a scam website

1. A domain name that looks almost like the real brand

Scammers swap one character (m becomes rn, o becomes 0), add words like -promo or -support, or use unusual extensions such as .xyz, .top or .cc. Read the domain slowly, character by character — don't just look at the logo.

2. The brand name comes first, but the real domain is at the end

In an address like yourbank.com.verify-account.top, the real domain is verify-account.top — everything before it is just decoration. The real domain is always the part right before the first /.

3. An HTTPS padlock doesn't mean the site is safe

The padlock icon only means the connection is encrypted; it doesn't prove the website is genuine. Most scam sites today use HTTPS.

4. It asks for your password, OTP or card number right away

Banks and government agencies don't text you links asking you to log in or read out an OTP. Any page that demands an OTP the moment it opens is almost certainly a scam.

5. Unbelievably low prices, huge discounts and urgent countdowns

"Only 5 minutes left", "90% off" and "You've won an iPhone" are pressure tactics designed to stop you from thinking.

6. Payment only by transfer to a personal bank account

If the account holder's name doesn't match the company name, or the site doesn't offer cash on delivery, be suspicious.

7. Thin contact details

No address, no tax ID, no customer service hotline — or just a single mobile number and a messaging-app account.

8. Typos, blurry logos and a patched-together design

Fake pages are usually copied in a hurry, so they often have spelling mistakes, broken images or buttons that don't work.

9. The link came from a text message, an ad or a random comment

If you didn't type the address yourself but tapped it from an SMS, a WhatsApp, Telegram or Messenger message or a scam ad, assume it's suspicious and check it carefully before entering any information.

Scammers also use more sophisticated tricks to make scam pages look "clean" when they're inspected — read more in 12 camouflage techniques scam websites use.

Look up a website in a public database

You can also look up a domain in Google Safe Browsing site status: type the address into the search box and it tells you whether Google currently flags the site as unsafe. Many countries also run an official scam-reporting service or cybercrime reporting portal where you can check or report suspicious websites.

Note: new scam sites appear every day, so a site not being on the list doesn't guarantee it's safe — combine the lookup with the 9 signs above.

What to do if you entered your details on a scam site

  1. Immediately call your bank's official hotline (the number on the back of your card) to lock your account and cards.
  2. Change the password you entered, plus the password of every other account that uses the same one.
  3. Turn on two-factor authentication for your email and social media.
  4. Report the scam site, and file a police report if you have lost money.

Conclusion

No single sign is 100% reliable, but when a website has a strange domain, asks for your OTP and pushes you to transfer money, it's almost certainly a scam. Make it a habit to check before entering any information — and let SafePhone do the checking automatically for less tech-savvy family members.

FAQ

How can I check whether a website is a scam?

Read the domain name carefully, and be suspicious if the site immediately asks for an OTP or password, offers unusually low prices, or only accepts transfers to a personal bank account. You can paste the link into SafePhone's URL check box or look up the domain in Google's Safe Browsing site status tool.

Is a website with an HTTPS padlock safe?

No. The HTTPS padlock only means the connection is encrypted; it does not prove the website is genuine. Most scam sites today use HTTPS.

If a domain isn't on a scam blacklist, is it safe?

Not necessarily. New scam sites appear every day and may not have been added to the list yet. Combine a lookup with checking for the warning signs.

What should I do if I entered my banking password on a scam website?

Call your bank's official hotline right away to lock your account and cards, change your password, turn on two-factor authentication, and report it to the police if you have lost money.
